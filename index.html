Two-Fan Regenerative Air-Braking System

A working model of a car braking system that captures energy from airflow while slowing down, stores it in a spring, and releases it as forward thrust when the car accelerates again.

How it works

Kinetic → elastic → mechanical

Instead of wasting braking energy as heat, the system turns the car's motion into stored spring energy, then gives it back as thrust.

Brake

A shield drops to expose the front turbine to the airflow. It creates drag and spins the drivetrain.

Kinetic energy

Store

Bevel gears turn the rotation 90°, and a pinion drives rack 1 to compress the spring.

→ Elastic potential

Hold

A small spring-powered stopper locks the mechanism, keeping the energy stored.

Stored

Release

When accelerating, the stopper is pulled. The spring drives rack 2 through a separate gear train and spins the rear fan as a propeller.

→ Mechanical thrust
INPUT

Front turbine

Catches the oncoming air during braking and turns drag into rotation.

TRANSMISSION

Bevel gears

Redirect the turbine's rotation by 90° into the drivetrain.

CONVERSION

Rack & pinion

Turn rotary motion into the straight-line motion that compresses the spring.

STORAGE

Compression spring

Holds the captured energy as elastic potential energy.

CONTROL

Spring-loaded stopper

Locks the compressed spring and releases it on demand.

OUTPUT

Rear fan

Driven by rack 2, it spins as a propeller to push the car forward.

Design story

From a past-paper question to a workshop

The hardest problem was turning the turbine's rotation into the straight-line motion needed to compress a spring. After seeing a rack-and-pinion mechanism in a past-paper question, Suban realised it could solve this and adapted it for the design.

He did the engineering calculations himself, using what he'd learned in class and researching extra formulas when he needed them. To turn the rotation by 90°, his brother, a mechanical engineer, introduced him to bevel gears. He used Claude to turn his drawings and descriptions into a 3D CAD model.

For fabrication, he worked with a mechanic near his home whose machining experience was invaluable. In that small workshop he learned to use lathes, gear shapers, milling machines and welding equipment. Together they made and assembled custom gears, shafts, stoppers and deep-groove ball bearings.

The build showed him the real problems of manufacturing and assembly, and how a design has to change once it leaves the calculations.
